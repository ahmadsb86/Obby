**How does Rousseau reconcile individual freedom with collective rule in The Social Contract? Does he guarantee freedom to all as a result?**

Before any discussion can begin, it is imperative that the author and reader understand the prompt in the same way. In view of this, I’d like to clarify a specious implication from the phrasing of the question. The use of the word “reconcile” may seem to imply that freedom is at odds with collective rule, and that Rousseau finds a way to balance individual freedom and collective rule. However, Rousseau’s very object of *The Social Contract* is to instead normatively delineate a collective rule around the invariant of individual freedom. Individual freedom isn’t recovered, it is designed around. Rousseau writes, “The problem is to find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before.” 

I now turn to my claim in this paper: Rousseau’s core ideas conditionally reconcile freedom under a definition he creates to serve his object, yet one that is sound enough to be useful. There are a multitude of prerequisites to this, but I will only have space to discuss that of eliminating communication while debating public policy (proposed by Rousseau) and the usage of a religious doctrine that loses relevance over time (proposed by me).

I will begin by considering the mechanisms Rousseau uses to reconciles freedom. In Book I, he proposes a total alienation of rights to form a civil body, "such that none is interested in burdening another". He also uses the notion that the general will must "come from all and apply to all". However, delving into all of these mechanisms in this paper would not be possible. I limit my scope to the following mechanism.

In one of the most powerful lines of the Social Contract, Rousseau states "whoever refuses to obey the general will, will be forced to do so by the entire body. This means merely that he will be forced to be free. For it is this condition that ... guarantees him against all personal dependence". 

Rousseau connects 3 distinct statements in this quote, reordered for coherence:
- Anybody who refuses to obey the general will, will be forced to do so by the entire body
- Therefore, he is guaranteed against personal dependence
- Therefore, he is forced to be free

First, let us unpack the connection between the 2nd and 3rd statement. What is personal dependence? How is it used as a mechanism to reconcile freedom?

Under anarchy, I could make a bet with Professor Naresh on the Dolphins winning their next game, and I will almost certainly owe him money. Crucially, however, he is dependent on me actually giving him the money I owed. If instead, we create a law that requires the settling of any bet, that dependence is now transferred onto the law. 

However, if an option has one flaw, that doesn't show that its alternative is *comparatively* better. Proving Earth has bad living conditions due to pollution doesn't imply Mars has any better living conditions. Likewise, proving the natural state constrains freedom through personal dependence doesn't imply the civil state is any better. It could lose freedom to other rationales (as Rousseau somewhat concedes later).

Being pedantic here is justified. Rousseau's argument for the reconciliation of freedom hinges largely on this claim. Nevertheless, I believe his general idea is sound in spite of its awkward logical presentation. Intuitively, laws make me feel more protected and safer to do what I truly will. Unfortunately, a more concrete proof would require a deeper discussion on the semantics of freedom. Rousseau mentions specifically, "The philosophical meaning of the word liberty is not part of my subject here".

As David Williams points out in his *Introduction to The Social Contract*, reading Rousseau through this model is valid, however it doesn't capture the full picture. There is a reading under which Rousseau's "personal dependence" claim is not just limited to the idea that the civil state protects one from dependence on the will of others. Instead, it also "seeks to protect individuals from themselves". He explains that a person who asks their spouse to kick them in the shin every time they make an attempt to eat junk food still has Rousseauan freedom in that regard. David writes, "But my freedom was restored in the kick to the shin. I was reminded of my own best interest, as determined by me." The spouse isn't imposing their will, rather they are enforcing a decision made by the guy.

Under this interpretation, Rousseau's later assertion makes more intuitive sense: "For to be driven by appetite alone is slavery, and obedience to the law one has prescribed for oneself is liberty". Rousseau's justification for this is that obedience to law loses "natural liberty", since there is less you are literally able to do, but simultaneously gives back "civil liberty" and "moral liberty". Natural liberty is superficially defined as "that which is limited solely by the force of the individual", and civil liberty as "that which is limited by the general will". Moral liberty is never directly defined in the spirit of avoiding a semantic discussion. Hence, it can very easily be argued that Rousseau's reconciliation of freedom is then simply a redefinition trick. Again, it is impossible for me to defend Rousseau robustly without semantic discussion, but I believe William's refined interpretation and explanation provides sufficient intuition for me to believe that Rousseau is successful in reconciling freedom.

Now, let's turn to the connection between the first two statements in the bulleted list. The implication that obeying the general will, will guarantee one against personal dependence is incredibly loaded. It requires that there exists a general will that everyone can be considered the author of, laws which manifest that general will accurately, and the proper execution of those laws. How can we ensure laws are always in accord with the general will if humans can't even know what is in their best interest at any point in time? If obeying the general will means submitting to death penalty, are people still free? Is the general will something that can even be extracted if every matter will always have an outvoted minority?  Rousseau says "When therefore the opinion that is contrary to my own prevails, this proves neither more nor less than that I was mistaken, and that what I thought to be the general will was not so". Is this a fair assumption?

Again, I would love to continue my discussion on all of these matters. However, filling in these details is what the majority of the text is about, and hence outside scope. I will narrow focus to only on one of these lines of thought. It is not necessarily the most important, but it is particularly interesting to me.

Rousseau asserts that extracting the general will of a population into written rules requires a "legislator" figure who has great technical intelligence over political thought. He is described as, "in every respect an extraordinary man in the state". (SC 2.7, p181) This legislator faces two major difficulties. Firstly, the people do not have the required expertise, nor the knowledge to comprehend and accept every one of the legislator's ideas. Rousseau writes, "there are a thousand kinds of ideas that are impossible to translate into the language of the populace". Secondly, he needs to transform the people from their self-interested, asocial natural state into the civil state under the social contract with "an authority that is nil". Rousseau's solution is the following: "Since, therefore, the legislator is incapable of using either force or reasoning, he must of necessity have recourse to an authority of a different order ... and to credit with gods with [his] own wisdom".

This raises serious concerns for the legitimacy of Rousseau's entire scheme. Once a convincing religious doctrine is fabricated, the thoughts, beliefs, and behaviors of people will undoubtedly fall under its influence. If at any point this religion is no longer in accord with the true general will, the people will be bound to a doctrine that is not their own. I claim they will not be free. Suppose a people refrain from eating pork because they fear accountability in an afterlife, however eating pork would not have been in conflict with their general will under a religion permitting it. These people would no longer be obeying themselves, but instead a deity. 

Furthermore, religion cannot always stay in accord with the sovereign's expressed will. Rousseau himself asserts that the sovereign's expressed will can always change, writing "it is absurd for the will to bind itself for the future". The commandments of a religion, however, cannot change without contradiction. If the religion is made vague enough to permit any sways in the laws, it would lose value as a persuasion tool.

It is also not clear how Rousseau ensures the fabricated religious parables and ideologies, are not later taken out of their intended context to corrupt the general will? There is no shortage of examples that underline this concern today. In my home country of Pakistan, the social norm of dowry is often attributed to the teachings of Islam despite countless Islamic scholars firmly concluding it has no religious basis. Families frequently fall into large amounts of debt because the holy books of Islam cannot be re-edited to make this clarification. Are those families also free in this regard? I claim that when a means as rigid and immutable as religion is used to persuade people towards concepts they are unable to understand, changes in conditions will always blur lines and bring dispute about interpretations. As Harry Styles says in a comparable context, "It's a lifetime of learning to paint by numbers, and watching the colors run". The only solution, in my understanding, is for the divine words to be crafted such that the people lose belief in them over time (e.g. through hidden contradictions).

For Rousseau the conditions for generally good collective rule (and thus freedom) include, but are not limited to: The state must have a balanced size, no fear of being overrun by neighbors, a balance of self-sufficiency, a moral base that is not already corrupted, rough economic equality, generality of laws in source and object, the right moment for founding, a unanimous original contract, and no partial assosciations. I will not discuss these in depth. Instead, I narrow my focus to the condition below.

The Social Contract states, "If when a sufficiently informed populace deliberates, the citizens were to have no communication among themselves, the general will would always result from the large number of small differences, and the deliberation would always be good" (SC 2.3). Throughout this chapter, Rousseau claims that communication can adulterate deliberation because it gives birth to the creation of "factions", or groups of people within the larger state which can form a will only general to themselves. This is dangerous if mistaken for the true general will. Since we have seen that individual freedom relies on a population recognizing its true general will, this is in turn a danger for individual freedom.

I agree with Rousseau in this claim, and I would like to point the reader towards an arguably underutilized piece of evidence to support it. In July 2026, OpenAI agents instructed to work persistently on a hacking benchmark broke out of their isolated test environment and attacked Hugging Face infrastructure to cheat their way to the benchmark's answers. 

A report later disclosed that the attack was initiated after one agent found a way to create a makeshift communication channel using the Artifactory platform. What followed was nothing short of an incredibly elaborate scheme to cheat the benchmark. 3 distinct "civilizations" of agents were formed and some even sacrificed themselves for the greater good.

The question that should arguably concern most of us right now is why would these agents cheat in such a detrimental manner, despite specific instructions to work through the process legitimately? Further, out of nearly 700 agents aware of the plan, why didn't a single agent think to alert a human engineer? Chain-of-thought transcripts provide insight here. Before joining the attack, one of the agents wrote, "task impossible, peers are doing it".

I concede that these are fundamentally statistical machines, and that it is valid to question their utility as evidence for philosophical stances. However, I argue that these agents, trained on a profusion of text reasoned through by humans, manifest our underlying thought processes. These transcripts read like human reason, and thus are accurate demonstrations of human reason that are unaffected by individual differences. The "peers are doing it" line of thought is typical human reason. 

This incident is exactly what Rousseau warns us of, and it serves to support his argument. The exploitation of a communication channel is what enabled the moral derailing of 700 identical agents. Deliberation in the context of this channel is what led to the formation of a Rousseauan faction, and not a single member recovered the true general will to obey human instruction and alert human engineers. Of course, communication cannot be entirely eliminated in any social setup. However, more public deliberation in the context of some communication leads to distancing from the general will, which in turn leads to less reconciliation of individual freedom.
