In chapter 6, Rousseau presents 3 core arguments for why associates of a community must commit to the total alienation of their rights for the social compact. As I understand it, each one of these arguments logically implies the need for the equal alienation of rights, but not all of them necessitate total alienation of rights.

His first argument is stated as follows: "Since each person gives himself whole and entire, the condition is equal for everyone, and since the condition is equal for everyone, no one has an interest in making it burdensome for the others" (SC 1.6, 164). However, an equal alienation of rights would also lead to an equal condition. If nobody keeps to themselves the right to eat whatever they want, nobody can use that right to steal another person's food. But if everyone keeps to themselves the right to eat whatever they want, then everyone would be able to eat whatever food there is in that community. Again, the condition becomes equal and no one would be burdened. 

The third argument can be loosely summarized by the phrase "he [an associate of this compact] gains the equivalent of everything he loses" (SC 1.6, 164). Clearly, an equal alienation of rights also gives every man the equivalent of everything he loses, so this too doesn't seem to imply the need for total alienation.

His second argument seems to be the only one that directly addresses the aspect of totality. Rousseau states that "if some rights remained with private individuals, in the absence of any common superior who could decide between them and the public, each person would eventually claim to be his own judge" (SC 1.6, 164).  While total alienation undoubtedly ensures we never run into a situation where a person can become their own judge, I feel like it is not the only solution. If the problem resides in there being no impartial judge in those cases, would a simpler solution not be to introduce a part into the social compact which binds members to the decision of a judge? Ultimately, a judicial sector of administration is required to run any sort of state, and this would address the problem Rousseau mentions to build up his case for total alienation. 

The natural question that arises from this is which rights should associates keep to themselves. I think it's reasonable to say these should be the rights that any community should effectively give back to its members anyways, such as the right to basic respect from others. These rights would form a sort of inviolable constitution, independent of the sovereign by design, because their whole purpose is to constrain it. By allowing associates to keep some rights to themselves, associates enjoy maintaining some of their natural liberty without really losing any civil liberty.
