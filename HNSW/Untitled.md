## The German Ideology (Tucker pp. 150-155, 172-174)

### pp. 150-155: Premises of the materialist method

- **Starting point:** real, living individuals and their material conditions, not abstractions, “the German” ideas, or what people imagine or say about themselves.
- **What distinguishes humans:** they begin to differ from animals when they _produce_ their means of subsistence. Production is how they indirectly produce their material life.
- **Mode of production = mode of life:** how people produce is what they are. Individuals depend on the material conditions that determine their production.
- **Production presupposes intercourse:** production requires exchange between individuals, and its form is shaped by the division of labour.
- **Division of labour and forms of property:** each stage of the division of labour corresponds to a form of ownership.
    - Tribal: weak division of labour, extension of the family.
    - Ancient communal/state: cities, slavery, emerging private property.
    - Feudal: land-based, serfs, guilds.
- **Basic historical moments (not stages):** producing the means to satisfy needs, new needs generated by satisfying old ones, reproducing the species (the family), and the social character of production. Consciousness and language arise from these as a social product, not as pure thought.

### pp. 172-174: Ruling class and ruling ideas

- **Core claim:** the class that holds material power is also the ruling intellectual force. It controls the means of mental production, so those without them are subject to its ideas.
- Ruling ideas are the ideal expression of the dominant material relationships.
- **Division within the ruling class:** its thinkers (ideologists) produce the class’s self-conception, while its active members are more passive about it. Any hostility between the two is minor and vanishes when the class itself is threatened.
- **Universality:** a class seeking to displace the old ruling class must present its interest as the common interest of society and give its ideas the form of universality, as the only rational, valid ones.
- **Consequence:** detaching ideas from the ruling class and its material conditions leads to the illusion that ideas rule history.

## Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts (pp. 66-81)

### Preface and framing (pp. 66-70)

- Marx plans a critical engagement with Hegel and with political economy, grounded in Feuerbach’s naturalism and humanism.
- Political economy takes private property as a given fact rather than explaining it.

### Estranged Labour (pp. 70-81)

- **Starting fact:** under private property the worker becomes poorer as he produces more wealth. The product confronts him as an alien power.
- **Four forms of alienation:**
    1. **From the product:** the object he makes is independent of and hostile to him. Labour is objectified, but that objectification appears as loss.
    2. **From the activity of labour:** work is external to him, forced, and not his own. He feels himself only outside work, and at work he is not at home.
    3. **From species-being:** humans are distinctively free, conscious, universal producers, but estranged labor reduces life activity to a mere means of individual survival.
    4. **From other humans:** if the product and activity belong to another, they belong to a person other than the worker, which sets up the capitalist-worker relation.
- **Private property as consequence, not cause:** private property results from estranged labour and then reinforces it, as the means by which labour is externalized.
- **Closing question (around p. 81):** how does the estrangement relate to the development of humanity? Marx suggests that emancipating society from private property takes the political form of worker emancipation.
